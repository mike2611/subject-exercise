# Take-home exercise: "What should I practice next?"

**Role:** Senior Full Stack Developer, Subject
**Time budget:** 3-6 hours. Hard stop at 6.
**Deliverable:** a repo (or zip) + a 20-minute presentation to the engineering team.
**Language:** English, for both the README and the presentation.

---

## Why we do it this way

We are not trying to find out whether you can grind. We want to see how you make
decisions when the problem is underspecified and the clock is real, because that is the
actual job. A small, sharply-reasoned submission beats a large, unfocused one every time.

**Please stop at six hours.** If you run out of time, write down what you would do next and
why. We read that section closely, and "I ran out of time and here is my plan" is a
perfectly strong answer. Submitting something unfinished with clear reasoning scores better
than submitting something finished that ignored the tradeoffs.

**Use AI tools if you want.** Copilot, Cursor, Claude, ChatGPT are all fine, and we use them
daily. The only rule is that you must be able to explain and defend every decision in the
code as your own. We will ask.

---

## The problem

Subject teaches students through short-form video and practice. A student finishes a
session and we want to answer one question well:

> **"What should I practice next, and why?"**

You have `attempts.json` - a raw export of practice attempts for three students across six
math skills. Build a thin vertical slice that answers that question for one student.

### What to build

**1. An API endpoint** - `GET /api/next-practice?student_id=...`

It should return the skill the student should work on next, plus a short, warm,
student-facing explanation of why.

The *selection* is yours to design. There is no right answer and we are far more interested
in your reasoning than your formula. Things you might weigh: accuracy per skill, recency,
time-on-task, whether hints were needed, how much evidence you actually have.

**2. An LLM call, with a fallback that always works**

The student-facing explanation should be generated by an LLM (any provider). Two hard
requirements:

- If the model is slow, errors, rate-limits, or returns something unusable, **the endpoint still returns a useful explanation.** Students are not allowed to see a spinner forever or an error page.
- The model's output must not be rendered blindly. Decide what "unusable" means and enforce it.

Keep the prompt in version control. We want to read it.

**3. One screen** - the student's view

A single mobile-width screen that shows the recommendation and the reason, and gives the
student a way to start. Assume a **low-end Android phone on an unreliable 3G connection**.
That constraint should visibly shape what you build, and you should be able to say how.

### What NOT to build

We mean this. Building these will count against you, not for you:

- Authentication, signup, or user management
- A database. Read the JSON file.
- An admin panel, a teacher view, or more than one screen
- Docker, CI, cloud deployment, infrastructure-as-code
- A component library or design system
- Exhaustive test coverage. Two or three tests on the part that most deserves them is right.

### Stack

Your choice. For reference, our stack is **TypeScript, Node, Next.js/React, PostgreSQL, GCP**,
and using it is a mild plus, but we would rather see excellent work in the tools you know
than shaky work in ours.

### About the data

`attempts.json` is a real-shaped export and, as the note in the file says, **it is not
guaranteed clean.** Handle what you find as you think best. If you make an assumption, write
it down.

One thing worth noticing: the amount of evidence per student per skill is very uneven. At
least one student has almost no history on one skill. What you do about that is one of the
more interesting decisions in this exercise.

## What to hand in

**1. The code**, with a README covering:

- How to run it (assume we have Node/Python and nothing else configured)
- **Your selection logic and why** - the tradeoffs you considered and rejected
- **What you did about LLM reliability** - failure modes you handled, and how you validate output
- **What you did about the phone and the 3G connection**
- **Cost.** Roughly what does one recommendation cost to serve at scale, and what would you do to bring that down at 100,000 students a day? A paragraph of honest estimation is fine; we are looking for whether you think about it at all.
- Your assumptions, and what you would do with another week

**2. A 20-minute presentation** to the engineering team. No slide-deck polish required -
demo it, walk us through the code, tell us what you would change. Then 20 minutes of
questions.

Suggested shape:
- 3 min - how you read the problem and what you chose to scope in and out
- 7 min - live demo, including what happens when the LLM fails
- 7 min - code walkthrough of the part you are proudest of and the part you are least happy with
- 3 min - what you would do next

---

## How we will assess it

We are looking at, roughly in this order:

1. **Judgment under a time budget** - did you spend your six hours on what mattered?
2. **Reliability thinking** - is the failure path real, or an afterthought?
3. **Product sense** - would a 14-year-old understand the explanation and want to click?
4. **Code quality** - clear boundaries, honest naming, no cleverness for its own sake
5. **Communication** - can you explain a tradeoff to someone who has not read your code?

We are explicitly *not* scoring: visual design, test coverage percentages, how many
features you fit in, or whether you picked our stack.

Questions before you start are welcome and count for you, not against you. Email
marco@subject.com.
